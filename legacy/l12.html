12.1 Harmonic functions with given boundary values

The lecture opens with a Dirichlet problem (Part I's question) that is hard on a disk and easy on a half-plane, and asks how to move it from one to the other.

DefinedExample 12.1.1 (the plate). A plate $D(0,1)$ has its upper edge held at temperature $1$ and its lower edge at $0$. The temperature solves the heat equation $u_t=\Delta u$; in equilibrium $u_t=0$, so $u$ is harmonic. Problem: find a harmonic $u$ on $D(0,1)$ with limit $1$ at each point $e^{i\theta}$, $0\lt\theta\lt\pi$, and limit $0$ at each point $e^{i\theta}$, $\pi\lt\theta\lt2\pi$.

The boundary data jump at $\pm1$, so this is not quite the continuous Dirichlet problem of Theorem 5.1.1; no value is prescribed at the two jump points.

DerivedProposition 12.1.2 (the same problem on the half-plane). On the upper half-plane $\mathbb H:=\{\operatorname{Im}z\gt0\}$, write $z=x+iy$ with $\arg z\in(0,\pi)$, and let \[ v(z):=\frac1\pi\arg z=\frac12-\frac1\pi\arctan\frac xy. \] Then $v$ is harmonic on $\mathbb H$, with limit $0$ at each point of $(0,\infty)$ and limit $1$ at each point of $(-\infty,0)$.
Proof.
  1. The two formulas agree. For $y\gt0$ the angle $\alpha=\arg z\in(0,\pi)$ has $\cot\alpha=x/y$. On $(0,\pi)$, $\alpha=\frac\pi2-\arctan(\cot\alpha)$ (both sides decrease from $\pi$ to $0$ and have the same cotangent). So $\frac1\pi\arg z=\frac12-\frac1\pi\arctan\frac xy$.
  2. Harmonic, by direct computation. Let $\phi:=\arctan\frac xy$. Then \[ \phi_x=\frac{1/y}{1+x^2/y^2}=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\qquad\phi_y=\frac{-x/y^2}{1+x^2/y^2}=\frac{-x}{x^2+y^2}, \] \[ \phi_{xx}=\frac{-2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2},\qquad\phi_{yy}=\frac{2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2}, \] so $\Delta\phi=0$, and $v=\frac12-\frac1\pi\phi$ is harmonic.
  3. Boundary limits. Let $z\to x_0\ne0$ from $\mathbb H$, so $y\to0^+$ and $x\to x_0$. Then $x/y\to+\infty$ if $x_0\gt0$ and $\to-\infty$ if $x_0\lt0$, so $\arctan\frac xy\to\pm\frac\pi2$ and $v\to0$, respectively $1$. ∎

The plan: carry $v$ from $\mathbb H$ to $D(0,1)$ by a bijection that keeps functions harmonic. Two ingredients are needed: holomorphic maps preserve harmonicity (§12.3), and an explicit holomorphic bijection $\mathbb H\to D(0,1)$ (§12.4).

12.2 Harmonic functions are real parts

DerivedLemma 12.2.1 (harmonic conjugate on a disk). Let $u$ be harmonic on a disk $D:=D(z_0,r)$, $z_0=x_0+iy_0$. Then $u=\operatorname{Re}F$ for some $F$ holomorphic on $D$.
Proof.
  1. Define the candidate imaginary part. For $x+iy\in D$ let \[ v(x,y):=\int_{y_0}^yu_x(x,t)\,dt-\int_{x_0}^xu_y(s,y_0)\,ds, \] integrating along the horizontal segment from $(x_0,y_0)$ to $(x,y_0)$ and then the vertical segment to $(x,y)$. Both segments lie in $D$: their points $(s,y_0)$ and $(x,t)$ are no farther from the centre than $(x,y)$ is, since $|s-x_0|\le|x-x_0|$ and $|t-y_0|\le|y-y_0|$.
  2. $v_y=u_x$. Only the first integral depends on $y$; by the fundamental theorem of calculus, $v_y=u_x(x,y)$.
  3. $v_x=-u_y$. Differentiate the first integral in $x$ under the integral sign (A.4; $u$ is $C^2$), and use $u_{xx}=-u_{yy}$: \[ v_x=\int_{y_0}^yu_{xx}(x,t)\,dt-u_y(x,y_0)=-\int_{y_0}^yu_{yy}(x,t)\,dt-u_y(x,y_0)=-\big(u_y(x,y)-u_y(x,y_0)\big)-u_y(x,y_0)=-u_y(x,y). \]
  4. Conclude. $F:=u+iv$ has $C^1$ real and imaginary parts satisfying $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$, so it is holomorphic by Theorem 1.5.2. ∎

This is Lecture 1's stream function again: there (Prop. 1.8.1, Poincaré lemma) a curl-free, divergence-free flow on a disk had both a velocity potential and a stream function. Here the field is $\nabla u$, and $v$ is its stream function, the harmonic conjugate of $u$.

Remark 12.2.2 (the disk matters). $\log|z|$ is harmonic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ (E.6), but it is not $\operatorname{Re}F$ for any $F$ holomorphic there. If it were, then, writing $F=U+iV$ with $U=\log|z|$, Theorem 1.5.2 and Cauchy–Riemann give $F'=U_x+iV_x=U_x-iU_y=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}=\frac1z$. The integral of a derivative around a closed curve is $0$ (it is $F(\text{end})-F(\text{start})$), so $\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}z=\oint_{|z|=1}F'(z)\,dz=0$, contradicting $\oint\frac{dz}z=2\pi i$ (Cor. 2.6.4). The hole at $0$ is what goes wrong; compare Prop. 1.14.3, where $\frac1z$ has no global primitive.

12.3 Harmonic functions under holomorphic maps

DerivedTheorem 12.3.1 (harmonic composed with holomorphic). Let $U,V$ be open, $f:U\to V$ holomorphic, and $u$ harmonic on $V$. Then $u\circ f$ is harmonic on $U$.
Proof. Harmonicity is a local property, so it suffices to check it near each $z_0\in U$. Let $D\subseteq V$ be a disk about $f(z_0)$. By Lemma 12.2.1, $u=\operatorname{Re}F$ on $D$ with $F$ holomorphic. The set $f^{-1}(D)$ is open (as $f$ is continuous) and contains $z_0$, and on it $u\circ f=\operatorname{Re}(F\circ f)$. The composition $F\circ f$ is holomorphic (chain rule), so its real part is harmonic (Cor. 6.6.2). ∎
DerivedCorollary 12.3.2 (transporting harmonic functions). If $f:U\to V$ is a bijection and $f$, $f^{-1}$ are holomorphic, then $u\mapsto u\circ f$ is a bijection from the harmonic functions on $V$ onto those on $U$, with inverse $h\mapsto h\circ f^{-1}$.
Proof. Theorem 12.3.1 applies to $f$ and to $f^{-1}$, and $(u\circ f)\circ f^{-1}=u$, $(h\circ f^{-1})\circ f=h$. ∎
DerivedProposition 12.3.3 (how the Laplacian transforms). For $f$ holomorphic and $u$ a $C^2$ function, $\Delta(u\circ f)=|f'|^2\,(\Delta u)\circ f$.
Proof. Write $f=p+iq$ and $u=u(p,q)$. By the chain rule, $\partial_x(u\circ f)=u_pp_x+u_qq_x$, and differentiating again, \[ \Delta(u\circ f)=u_{pp}(p_x^2+p_y^2)+2u_{pq}(p_xq_x+p_yq_y)+u_{qq}(q_x^2+q_y^2)+u_p\,\Delta p+u_q\,\Delta q. \] By Cauchy–Riemann, $p_x=q_y$ and $p_y=-q_x$. Then $p_xq_x+p_yq_y=q_yq_x-q_xq_y=0$; $p_x^2+p_y^2=q_x^2+q_y^2=|f'|^2$ (as $f'=p_x+iq_x$); and $\Delta p=\Delta q=0$ (Prop. 2.8.1). What remains is $|f'|^2(u_{pp}+u_{qq})$. ∎

So the equation $\Delta u=0$ is preserved, which is all that equilibria need. The heat equation $u_t=\Delta u$ is not: it becomes $u_t=|f'|^{-2}\Delta u$, heat flow in a material whose conductivity varies from point to point. Only the equilibrium problem transfers.

12.4 The Cayley map

DerivedProposition 12.4.1 (Cayley map). $C(z):=\dfrac{z-i}{z+i}$ is a bijection of $\mathbb H$ onto $D(0,1)$, with inverse $C^{-1}(w)=i\,\dfrac{1+w}{1-w}$. Both are holomorphic.
Proof.
  1. $C$ maps $\mathbb H$ into the disk. $|C(z)|\lt1\iff|z-i|\lt|z+i|$: $z$ is closer to $i$ than to $-i$. With $z=x+iy$, \[ |z-i|^2-|z+i|^2=\big(x^2+(y-1)^2\big)-\big(x^2+(y+1)^2\big)=-4y, \] so this holds exactly when $y\gt0$.
  2. Solve for $z$. For $w\ne1$, \[ \frac{z-i}{z+i}=w\iff z-i=wz+wi\iff z(1-w)=i(1+w)\iff z=i\,\frac{1+w}{1-w}. \]
  3. Onto. For $|w|\lt1$, this $z$ satisfies $C(z)=w$, so $|C(z)|\lt1$ and, by step 1, $z\in\mathbb H$. So each $w\in D(0,1)$ has exactly one preimage in $\mathbb H$.
  4. Holomorphic. Both are rational functions; their only poles, $z=-i$ and $w=1$, lie outside $\mathbb H$ and outside $D(0,1)$. ∎

The real line goes to the unit circle: for real $x$, $|x-i|=|x+i|$. And $C(i)=0$, $C(0)=-1$, $C(\infty)=1$.

12.5 Solution of the problem

DerivedProposition 12.5.1 (the plate solved). $u:=v\circ C^{-1}$ solves Example 12.1.1. Explicitly, \[ u(w)=\frac1\pi\arg\Big(i\,\frac{1+w}{1-w}\Big)=\frac12+\frac1\pi\arg\frac{1+w}{1-w},\qquad\arg\in(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2), \] and $u(0)=\frac12$.
Proof.
  1. Harmonic. By Theorem 12.3.1 applied to $C^{-1}:D(0,1)\to\mathbb H$, $u=v\circ C^{-1}$ is harmonic on $D(0,1)$.
  2. Where the boundary goes. Using $e^{\pm i\theta/2}$, \[ \frac{1+e^{i\theta}}{1-e^{i\theta}}=\frac{e^{-i\theta/2}+e^{i\theta/2}}{e^{-i\theta/2}-e^{i\theta/2}}=\frac{2\cos\frac\theta2}{-2i\sin\frac\theta2}=i\cot\frac\theta2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad C^{-1}(e^{i\theta})=-\cot\frac\theta2. \] This is negative for $0\lt\theta\lt\pi$ and positive for $\pi\lt\theta\lt2\pi$.
  3. Limits. $C^{-1}$ is continuous at $e^{i\theta}$ ($\theta\ne0$). So as $w\to e^{i\theta}$ inside the disk, $C^{-1}(w)\to-\cot\frac\theta2$ from inside $\mathbb H$, and by Prop. 12.1.2 $u(w)=v(C^{-1}(w))\to1$ on the upper half-circle and $\to0$ on the lower one.
  4. Explicit formula. $\frac{1+w}{1-w}=\frac{(1+w)(1-\bar w)}{|1-w|^2}=\frac{1-|w|^2+(w-\bar w)}{|1-w|^2}$ has real part $\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}\gt0$, so its argument lies in $(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)$, and multiplying by $i$ adds $\frac\pi2$. At $w=0$: $\arg(i)=\frac\pi2$, so $u(0)=\frac12$. ∎

Check: $u(0)=\frac12$ is the average of the boundary values (half the circle at $1$, half at $0$), as the mean value property (Theorem 4.2.2) demands. The level curves $\arg\frac{1+w}{1-w}=\text{const}$ are arcs of circles through $\pm1$, because the Möbius map $w\mapsto\frac{1+w}{1-w}$ sends them to rays from $0$.

Other regions need other holomorphic bijections, and each needs a holomorphic inverse for Cor. 12.3.2 to apply. That inverse comes for free: the inverse theorem, §12.9.

12.6 Local behaviour

DerivedTheorem 12.6.1 (local behaviour). Let $f$ be holomorphic near $z_0$, $w_0:=f(z_0)$, and let $f-w_0$ have a zero of order $m\ge1$ at $z_0$. Then for every small enough $r\gt0$ there is $\delta\gt0$ such that for each $w\in D(w_0,\delta)$, $f-w$ has exactly $m$ zeros in $D(z_0,r)$, counted with multiplicity.

So near $z_0$, $f$ behaves like $w_0+a_m(z-z_0)^m$, which takes each value $w$ near $w_0$ exactly $m$ times (the $m$ roots of $(z-z_0)^m=\frac{w-w_0}{a_m}$).

Proof.
  1. Taylor. The Taylor coefficients of $f-w_0$ at $z_0$ vanish below order $m$, and $a_m\ne0$. By Cor. 6.5.1 there are $\rho,C\gt0$ with \[ f(z)-w=\big[a_m(z-z_0)^m-(w-w_0)\big]+E(z),\qquad|E(z)|\le C|z-z_0|^{m+1}\ \ (|z-z_0|\le\rho). \]
  2. Choose $r$ and $\delta$. Take $r\le\rho$ with $Cr\lt\frac{|a_m|}2$, and $\delta:=\frac{|a_m|r^m}2$. Let $|w-w_0|\lt\delta$.
  3. The model has $m$ zeros in the disk. The bracket vanishes when $(z-z_0)^m=\frac{w-w_0}{a_m}$: $m$ roots (one root of order $m$ if $w=w_0$), all of modulus $\big(\frac{|w-w_0|}{|a_m|}\big)^{1/m}\lt\big(\frac{r^m}2\big)^{1/m}\lt r$.
  4. Compare on the circle. On $|z-z_0|=r$, \[ \big|a_m(z-z_0)^m-(w-w_0)\big|\ge|a_m|r^m-|w-w_0|\gt\frac{|a_m|r^m}2\gt Cr^{m+1}\ge|E(z)|, \] using $Cr\lt\frac{|a_m|}2$ in the third step.
  5. Rouché. By Theorem 11.10.1, with the bracket in the role of $f$ and $f-w$ in the role of $g$ (their difference is $E$), $f-w$ has as many zeros in $D(z_0,r)$ as the bracket: $m$. ∎

12.7 The open mapping theorem

DerivedTheorem 12.7.1 (open mapping). Let $f$ be holomorphic and not constant on a connected open set $U$. Then $f(U)$ is open.

That is: if $w_0=f(z_0)$, every $w$ close enough to $w_0$ is also a value of $f$. This fails for real functions: $x^2$ takes the value $0$ but no value $\lt0$. In contrast, every $w\in\mathbb C$ is a value of $z^2$.

Proof. Let $z_0\in U$ and $w_0:=f(z_0)$. If all Taylor coefficients of $f-w_0$ at $z_0$ vanished, $f\equiv w_0$ on a disk about $z_0$, hence on $U$ by the identity theorem (Theorem 10.1.1): but $f$ is not constant. So $f-w_0$ has a zero of finite order $m\ge1$ at $z_0$, and by Theorem 12.6.1, $D(w_0,\delta)\subseteq f(D(z_0,r))\subseteq f(U)$ for some $\delta\gt0$. ∎
Remark 12.7.2 (maximum modulus, again). If $|f|$ had a maximum at an interior point $z_0$, then $f(U)$ would contain a disk about $f(z_0)$, which contains points of larger modulus. So a non-constant holomorphic function on a connected open set has no interior maximum of $|f|$. This is a second proof of Lemma 9.6.1, and it is used that way in §12.13–§12.14 and §13.5.

12.8 Local injectivity

DerivedCorollary 12.8.1 (local injectivity). Let $f$ be holomorphic near $z_0$, and not constant near $z_0$. Then $f$ is injective on some disk about $z_0$ if and only if $f'(z_0)\ne0$.
Proof. Apply Theorem 12.6.1 with $w_0:=f(z_0)$ and $m$ the order of the zero of $f-w_0$ at $z_0$. Note that $m=1$ exactly when $a_1=f'(z_0)\ne0$.
  • (⇐) $f'(z_0)\ne0$, so $m=1$. Each $w\in D(w_0,\delta)$ is taken exactly once on $D(z_0,r)$. So $f$ is injective on $D(z_0,r)\cap f^{-1}(D(w_0,\delta))$: if $f(z_1)=f(z_2)=w$ for two points there, then $w\in D(w_0,\delta)$ is taken twice in $D(z_0,r)$ unless $z_1=z_2$. That set is open (an intersection of open sets) and contains $z_0$, so it contains a disk about $z_0$.
  • (⇒) If $f'(z_0)=0$ then $m\ge2$. $f'$ is not identically zero near $z_0$ ($f$ is not constant), so its zeros are isolated (Theorem 6.7.1): take $r$ so small that also $f'\ne0$ on $0\lt|z-z_0|\lt r$. For $w\in D(w_0,\delta)$ with $w\ne w_0$, the $m$ zeros of $f-w$ in $D(z_0,r)$ are not at $z_0$ (where $f=w_0\ne w$), so the derivative $f'$ is nonzero there and each zero is simple. So $f=w$ at $m\ge2$ distinct points of $D(z_0,r)$. As $r$ can be taken arbitrarily small, $f$ is injective on no disk about $z_0$. ∎

Example: $z^2$ at $0$ has derivative $0$, and indeed it takes each nearby $w\ne0$ twice, at $\pm\sqrt w$.

12.9 The inverse theorem; conformal maps

DerivedTheorem 12.9.1 (inverse). Let $f$ be holomorphic and injective on an open set $U$. Then $f'\ne0$ on $U$, $V:=f(U)$ is open, and $g:=f^{-1}$ is holomorphic on $V$, with $g'(f(z))=1/f'(z)$.
Proof.
  1. $f'\ne0$. Being injective, $f$ is not constant near any point, and it is injective on every disk, so $f'\ne0$ everywhere by Cor. 12.8.1.
  2. $V$ open and $g$ continuous. Apply the open mapping theorem on disks inside $U$ (each disk is connected): $f$ maps every open subset $W\subseteq U$ to an open set, a union of open images of disks. With $W=U$, $V$ is open. And $g^{-1}(W)=f(W)$ is open for every open $W$, which is continuity of $g$.
  3. Derivative. Let $w_1=f(z_1)\in V$ and $w=f(z)\to w_1$, $w\ne w_1$. Then $z=g(w)\to z_1$ (continuity of $g$), $z\ne z_1$ (injectivity), and \[ \frac{g(w)-g(w_1)}{w-w_1}=\frac{z-z_1}{f(z)-f(z_1)}\longrightarrow\frac1{f'(z_1)}, \] which exists since $f'(z_1)\ne0$. So $g$ is complex differentiable, and $g'=1/(f'\circ g)$ is continuous: $g$ is holomorphic with $C^1$ parts. ∎

In real analysis the first claim fails: $x^3$ is injective with derivative $0$ at $0$, and its inverse $\sqrt[3]x$ is not differentiable there.

DefinedDefinition 12.9.2 (conformal map). For $U,V$ open, a conformal map of $U$ onto $V$ is a holomorphic bijection $f:U\to V$. By Theorem 12.9.1, $f^{-1}$ is then a conformal map of $V$ onto $U$, and by Cor. 12.3.2 it transports harmonic functions both ways. Compositions of conformal maps are conformal maps (chain rule).

12.10 Conformal maps preserve angles

DerivedProposition 12.10.1 (angles). Let $f$ be holomorphic near $z_0$ with $f'(z_0)\ne0$, and let $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ be $C^1$ curves with $\gamma_j(0)=z_0$ and $\gamma_j'(0)\ne0$. Then \[ (f\circ\gamma_j)'(0)=f'(z_0)\,\gamma_j'(0). \] So both tangent vectors are multiplied by the same complex number $f'(z_0)$: rotated by $\arg f'(z_0)$ and scaled by $|f'(z_0)|$. The image curves meet at the same angle as $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$, and with the same orientation.
Proof. By complex differentiability, \[ f(\gamma_j(t))-f(z_0)=f'(z_0)\big(\gamma_j(t)-z_0\big)+o(|\gamma_j(t)-z_0|). \] Divide by $t$ and let $t\to0$: $\frac{\gamma_j(t)-z_0}t\to\gamma_j'(0)$, and the error term divided by $t$ tends to $0$ because $|\gamma_j(t)-z_0|/|t|$ stays bounded. The angle between the images is $\arg\frac{(f\circ\gamma_2)'(0)}{(f\circ\gamma_1)'(0)}=\arg\frac{\gamma_2'(0)}{\gamma_1'(0)}$, the same as before. ∎

This is where the word "conformal" (same form) comes from: tiny shapes are rotated and scaled, not distorted. Lecture 1's flows illustrate it: streamlines and equipotentials meet at right angles because both are images of the horizontal and vertical lines under a conformal map.

DefinedExample 12.10.2 ($z^2$ doubles angles at $0$). At a zero of $f'$ angles are not preserved: $z^2$ sends the ray $\arg z=\alpha$ to the ray $\arg z=2\alpha$, so it maps the quadrant $\{\operatorname{Re}z\gt0,\operatorname{Im}z\gt0\}$ onto $\mathbb H$. Away from $0$ angles are preserved: with $z^2=(x^2-y^2)+2ixy$, the line $\operatorname{Re}z=a$ goes to the parabola $u=a^2-\frac{v^2}{4a^2}$ and the line $\operatorname{Im}z=b$ to $u=\frac{v^2}{4b^2}-b^2$, and these parabolas still meet at right angles.

12.11 Examples: a strip

DerivedExample 12.11.1 (the strip). Let $S:=\{0\lt\operatorname{Im}z\lt\pi\}$. Then $e^z$ is a conformal map of $S$ onto $\mathbb H$, with inverse \[ L(z)=\log|z|+i\arg z,\qquad\arg z\in(0,\pi), \] holomorphic on $\mathbb H$. And $C(e^z)$ is a conformal map of $S$ onto $D(0,1)$.
Proof. $e^{x+iy}=e^xe^{iy}$ has modulus $e^x\in(0,\infty)$ and argument $y\in(0,\pi)$. Every point of $\mathbb H$ has a unique modulus in $(0,\infty)$ and a unique argument in $(0,\pi)$, each pair coming from exactly one $(x,y)$: so $e^z$ is a bijection $S\to\mathbb H$. It is holomorphic, so its inverse is holomorphic by Theorem 12.9.1, and solving $e^{x+iy}=z$ gives $x=\log|z|$, $y=\arg z$. Composing with the Cayley map (Prop. 12.4.1) gives a conformal map onto the disk. ∎

Horizontal lines $y=c$ go to rays from $0$ at angle $c$; vertical segments $x=c$ go to half-circles of radius $e^c$ about $0$.

12.12 Examples: the exterior of the disk

DerivedProposition 12.12.1 (Joukowski map). $J(z):=z+\dfrac1z$ is a conformal map of $\{|z|\gt1\}$ onto $\mathbb C\setminus[-2,2]$.
Proof.
  1. A quadratic. For $z\ne0$, $J(z)=w\iff z^2-wz+1=0$. Its two roots $z_1,z_2$ (counted with multiplicity) have $z_1z_2=1$, the constant term. So either exactly one of them has modulus $\gt1$ (and the other $\lt1$), or both have modulus $1$.
  2. Both on the unit circle exactly when $w\in[-2,2]$. If $|z_1|=1$, then $z_2=1/z_1=\bar z_1$, so $w=z_1+z_2=z_1+\bar z_1=2\operatorname{Re}z_1\in[-2,2]$. Conversely $w=2\cos\theta$ has the roots $e^{\pm i\theta}$, both of modulus $1$.
  3. Conclude. For $w\notin[-2,2]$, exactly one root lies in $\{|z|\gt1\}$: $J$ takes $w$ exactly once there. For $w\in[-2,2]$, no root lies there. So $J$ is a bijection of $\{|z|\gt1\}$ onto $\mathbb C\setminus[-2,2]$, and it is holomorphic. ∎

Circles go to ellipses: $J(re^{i\theta})=\big(r+\tfrac1r\big)\cos\theta+i\big(r-\tfrac1r\big)\sin\theta$, an ellipse with semi-axes $r\pm\frac1r$ and foci at $\pm\sqrt{(r+\frac1r)^2-(r-\frac1r)^2}=\pm2$. Rays go to hyperbola branches with the same foci. As $r\downarrow1$ the ellipses flatten onto the slit $[-2,2]$.

A circle through $1$ that encloses $-1$ goes to an airfoil with a sharp trailing edge at $J(1)=2$, because $J'(1)=0$ there and the angle is doubled (Example 12.10.2). These are Joukowski's wing profiles: flows past a cylinder (Example 1.15.1) are carried to flows past a wing.

12.13 Automorphisms of the disk

DefinedDefinition 12.13.1 (automorphism; $\varphi_a$). An automorphism of $D(0,1)$ is a conformal map of $D(0,1)$ onto itself. For $|a|\lt1$ let \[ \varphi_a(z):=\frac{a-z}{1-\bar az}. \]
DerivedLemma 12.13.2. $\varphi_a$ is an automorphism of $D(0,1)$, with $\varphi_a(0)=a$, $\varphi_a(a)=0$, and $\varphi_a\circ\varphi_a=\mathrm{id}$.
Proof.
  1. Holomorphic near the closed disk. The only pole is $z=1/\bar a$, with $|1/\bar a|\gt1$ (none if $a=0$, when $\varphi_0(z)=-z$). So $\varphi_a$ is holomorphic on $|z|\lt1/|a|$, which contains $\overline{D(0,1)}$.
  2. Modulus $1$ on the circle. For $|z|=1$, $z\bar z=1$, so $1-\bar az=z\bar z-\bar az=z(\bar z-\bar a)$, and $|1-\bar az|=|\bar z-\bar a|=|a-z|$. Hence $|\varphi_a(z)|=1$.
  3. Into the disk. By maximum modulus (Lemma 9.6.1), $|\varphi_a|\le1$ on the closed disk; and $|\varphi_a|\lt1$ inside, since a non-constant holomorphic function has no interior maximum of its modulus (Remark 12.7.2).
  4. Values. $\varphi_a(0)=a$ and $\varphi_a(a)=0$ by substitution.
  5. Its own inverse. Multiply numerator and denominator of $\varphi_a(\varphi_a(z))=\frac{a-\varphi_a(z)}{1-\bar a\varphi_a(z)}$ by $1-\bar az$: \[ \varphi_a(\varphi_a(z))=\frac{a(1-\bar az)-(a-z)}{(1-\bar az)-\bar a(a-z)}=\frac{(1-|a|^2)\,z}{1-|a|^2}=z. \] So $\varphi_a$ maps the disk into itself and is its own inverse there: a bijection, holomorphic both ways. ∎

So the disk "looks the same from every point": any point $a$ can be moved to the centre by an automorphism. That is what makes the disk a good standard model.

12.14 The Schwarz lemma

The maps $e^{i\theta}\varphi_a$ turn out to be all the automorphisms of the disk. The tool for proving this:

DerivedTheorem 12.14.1 (Schwarz lemma). Let $f$ be holomorphic on $D(0,1)$, with $|f|\lt1$ and $f(0)=0$. Then $|f(z)|\le|z|$ for $z\in D(0,1)$, and $|f'(0)|\le1$. If $|f(z_0)|=|z_0|$ for some $z_0\ne0$, or $|f'(0)|=1$, then $f(z)=cz$ with $|c|=1$: a rotation.
Proof.
  1. Divide by $z$. As $f(0)=0$, $\frac{f(z)}z=\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}\to f'(0)$ as $z\to0$, so $g(z):=f(z)/z$ is bounded near $0$ and extends holomorphically to $D(0,1)$ with $g(0)=f'(0)$ (Lemma 9.1.1).
  2. Bound on circles. On $|z|=r\lt1$, $|g|=|f|/r\lt1/r$. By maximum modulus (Lemma 9.6.1) on the closed disk of radius $r$, $|g|\le1/r$ on $D(0,r)$.
  3. Let $r\to1$. For fixed $z$, the bound holds for every $r\in(|z|,1)$, so $|g(z)|\le1$. That is, $|f(z)|\le|z|$, and $|f'(0)|=|g(0)|\le1$.
  4. Equality. If $|f(z_0)|=|z_0|$ with $z_0\ne0$, or $|f'(0)|=1$, then $|g|$ reaches its maximum value $1$ at an interior point ($z_0$, or $0$). By Remark 12.7.2, $g$ is a constant $c$, $|c|=1$, and $f(z)=cz$. ∎

The idea: a self-map of the disk that fixes $0$ cannot push any point outwards. All three practice midterms use this, usually after composing with $\varphi_a$ or the Cayley map to move a given point to $0$.

12.15 All automorphisms of the disk

DerivedTheorem 12.15.1 (automorphisms of the disk). The automorphisms of $D(0,1)$ are exactly the maps $z\mapsto e^{i\theta}\varphi_a(z)$, with $\theta\in\R$ and $|a|\lt1$.
Proof.
  1. These are automorphisms: by Lemma 12.13.2, followed by the rotation $w\mapsto e^{i\theta}w$.
  2. Move the problem to $0$. Let $\varphi$ be an automorphism and $a:=\varphi^{-1}(0)$. Then $\psi:=\varphi\circ\varphi_a$ is an automorphism with $\psi(0)=\varphi(a)=0$, and so is $\psi^{-1}$ (holomorphic by Theorem 12.9.1), also fixing $0$.
  3. Schwarz both ways. Theorem 12.14.1 for $\psi$ and for $\psi^{-1}$ gives \[ |\psi(z)|\le|z|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|z|=|\psi^{-1}(\psi(z))|\le|\psi(z)|. \] So $|\psi(z)|=|z|$ for every $z$, and by the equality case $\psi(z)=e^{i\theta}z$.
  4. Undo the move. As $\varphi_a\circ\varphi_a=\mathrm{id}$, $\varphi=\psi\circ\varphi_a=e^{i\theta}\varphi_a$. ∎

12.16 Liouville's theorem

Not every open set $U$ has a conformal map onto $D(0,1)$.

DerivedTheorem 12.16.1 (Liouville). A bounded entire function is constant.
Proof. Let $|f|\le M$ and $f(z)=\sum_na_nz^n$ (an entire function is one power series, Theorem 6.4.1). Cauchy's estimate (Cor. 6.5.2) on $|z|=R$ gives $|a_n|\le MR^{-n}$ for every $R\gt0$. Letting $R\to\infty$, $a_n=0$ for $n\ge1$. ∎
DerivedCorollary 12.16.2 (fundamental theorem of algebra). Every non-constant polynomial $p$ has a zero in $\mathbb C$.
Proof. Otherwise $1/p$ is entire. It is bounded: $|p(z)|\to\infty$ as $|z|\to\infty$ (the leading term dominates), so $|1/p|\le1$ outside some disk, and $1/p$ is bounded on that compact disk by continuity. By Liouville $1/p$ is constant, and so is $p$. ∎
DerivedCorollary 12.16.3. There is no conformal map of $\mathbb C$ onto $D(0,1)$.
Proof. It would be a bounded entire function, hence constant, hence not a bijection. ∎
Remark 12.16.4 (growth bounds, the same proof). The proof of Liouville gives more. If $f$ is entire and $|f(z)|\le C(1+|z|)^k$, then Cauchy's estimate on $|z|=R$ gives $|a_n|\le C(1+R)^kR^{-n}\to0$ for $n\gt k$: $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $k$. This is the tool for Problem 2(a) of the practice midterms. (Lemma 10.6.1 is the version for $\log|f|$.)

12.17 The Riemann mapping theorem

DefinedDefinition 12.17.1 (simply connected). An open set $U\subseteq\mathbb C$ is simply connected if it is connected and each connected component of $\mathbb C\setminus U$ is unbounded. (This is equivalent to the usual definition, "every closed curve in $U$ can be shrunk to a point within $U$".) Examples: disks, half-planes, strips, $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$. Not simply connected: the annulus $\{1\lt|z|\lt2\}$, whose complement has the bounded component $\{|z|\le1\}$.
CitedTheorem 12.17.2 (Riemann mapping theorem; stated). Let $U\ne\mathbb C$ be a nonempty, simply connected open set, and $z_0\in U$. There is exactly one conformal map $f$ of $U$ onto $D(0,1)$ with $f(z_0)=0$ and $f'(z_0)\gt0$.

Lecture 13 proves the existence for regions made of grid squares. Three comments from the slide:

  • $U\ne\mathbb C$ is needed, by Cor. 12.16.3.
  • Uniqueness (proved here, Prop. 12.17.3).
  • Boundary behaviour. If $\partial U$ is a Jordan curve (a closed curve without self-intersections), $f$ extends to a homeomorphism of $\overline U$ onto $\overline{D(0,1)}$ (Carathéodory's theorem, cited). So the Dirichlet problem on $U$ becomes one on the disk: $h\mapsto h\circ f$, by Cor. 12.3.2.
DerivedProposition 12.17.3 (uniqueness). Two conformal maps $f,g$ of $U$ onto $D(0,1)$ with $f(z_0)=g(z_0)=0$ and $f'(z_0),g'(z_0)\gt0$ are equal.
Proof. $g\circ f^{-1}$ is an automorphism of the disk (Theorem 12.9.1) fixing $0$. By Theorem 12.15.1 it is $e^{i\theta}\varphi_a$, and $e^{i\theta}\varphi_a(0)=e^{i\theta}a=0$ forces $a=0$; as $\varphi_0(w)=-w$, $g\circ f^{-1}(w)=cw$ with $|c|=1$. So $g=cf$, and $g'(z_0)=cf'(z_0)$ with both derivatives positive: $c\gt0$, so $c=1$. ∎
